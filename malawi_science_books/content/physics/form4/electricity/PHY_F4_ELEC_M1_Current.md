# The Big Idea

Electric current is **electric charge in motion**. Nothing more. When charge flows through a wire, we say there is a current. If charge does not flow — no matter how much "electricity" sits in the battery — there is no current. **A current only exists while charge is moving through a complete circuit.**

---

# Why Students Struggle

Three reasons, and they repeat every year:

1. Students say "current is used up" as it goes round a circuit. It is **not**. Current is the same at every point in a single loop.
2. Students confuse **current** (flow of charge) with **voltage** (energy per charge) with **resistance** (opposition to flow). These are three different things.
3. Students think the battery "gives current." The battery gives **energy** (voltage). The current is set by both the voltage and the resistance of the circuit.

Read these three again. If you fix only these, you fix most of your Electricity problems.

# Deep Explanation

## 1.1 What actually moves in a circuit?

Inside a metal wire there are **free electrons** — tiny negative particles that are loosely held by the metal atoms. They are always jiggling about randomly. On their own, they do not move anywhere as a group.

When you connect a battery, the battery creates an **electric field** along the wire. This field pushes the free electrons in one direction. They drift slowly along the wire — about a millimetre per second on average — but because there are so many of them, the *effect* is immediate: the bulb lights the instant you close the switch.

**Key idea:** the electrons themselves are slow, but the "push" travels through the wire at nearly the speed of light.

## 1.2 Electric charge

Charge is a property of matter, measured in **coulombs (C)**.

- One electron carries a charge of $-1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$.
- One coulomb is the charge on about $6.25 \times 10^{18}$ electrons.

Charge is given the symbol **Q**.

## 1.3 Electric current

**Current is the rate of flow of charge.**

$$I = \frac{Q}{t}$$

Where:
- $I$ = current in amperes (A)
- $Q$ = charge in coulombs (C)
- $t$ = time in seconds (s)

So **1 ampere = 1 coulomb per second**.

Rearranged:
- $Q = I \times t$ (useful for energy-bill-type questions)
- $t = Q / I$

## 1.4 Conventional current vs electron flow

Here is a fact that trips up students every year:

- **Electrons** flow from the **negative** terminal to the **positive** terminal.
- **Conventional current** is drawn as flowing from **positive** to **negative**.

Why? Because when scientists first studied electricity, they didn't know electrons existed. They guessed the moving charges were positive, and drew arrows that way. When electrons were discovered, the convention was already used everywhere. It is still used today.

**In exams, unless a question says otherwise, current arrows mean conventional current (positive → negative).**

You will not lose marks for using conventional current. You *will* lose marks if you mix them up in an explanation.

## 1.5 The complete circuit

For current to flow, there must be a **complete loop** from one terminal of the battery, through the components, back to the other terminal.

If the loop is broken anywhere — a loose wire, an open switch, a blown bulb — **no current flows anywhere in the circuit**. Not "less current." **Zero.**

This is why a series circuit with a blown bulb stops working entirely.

## 1.6 Open and closed circuits

- **Open circuit:** the loop is broken. No current. Ammeter reads 0 A.
- **Closed circuit:** the loop is complete. Current flows. Ammeter reads a value.

A switch is simply a controlled break in the circuit. When you flick a switch, you are not "turning on the electricity" — you are **completing or breaking the loop**.

## 1.7 Direction, size, and measuring current

- **Direction:** conventional current flows from + to − around the circuit.
- **Size:** measured in amperes. Small currents in milliamperes (1 mA = 0.001 A).
- **Measured with:** an **ammeter**, connected **in series** with the component you want to measure.

**Why in series?** Because the ammeter must have *all* the current pass through it to measure it. If you put it in parallel, you short the circuit and may damage the meter. (This is a favourite exam trap.)

## 1.8 Current is not "used up"

A very common wrong answer in MSCE scripts:

> "The current decreases as it goes round the circuit because the bulb uses it up."

**Wrong.** Current is not energy. In a single loop (series circuit), the **current is the same at every point** — before the bulb, through the bulb, after the bulb, back at the battery. What the bulb "uses up" is **energy**, delivered by the voltage. Not current.

If you remember nothing else from this module, remember this.

## 1.9 What is current, really, in one sentence?

> **Current is the flow of electric charge — measured as the number of coulombs passing a point per second — and in a series circuit it is the same everywhere.**

# Key Points Box

**Charge & current**
- Charge is measured in coulombs (C).
- Current is the rate of flow of charge: $I = Q/t$.
- 1 A = 1 C/s.
- Charge on one electron = $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

**What actually moves**
- Free electrons drift through the metal wire.
- Electrons flow from − to +.
- Conventional current is drawn from + to −.

**Circuits**
- A complete loop is required for any current to flow.
- Open circuit → 0 A. Closed circuit → current flows.
- A switch just opens or closes the loop.

**Current in a series circuit**
- Current is the **same** at every point in the loop.
- Current is **never used up** by components.
- Bulbs use **energy**, not current.

**Measuring current**
- Ammeter measures current.
- Ammeter goes **in series** with the component.
- Ammeter has very low resistance so it doesn't disturb the circuit.

**Units**
- 1 mA = 0.001 A
- 1 µA = 0.000001 A

# Worked Examples

## Worked Example 1 (Easy)

**Question:** A charge of 30 C flows past a point in a wire in 10 s. Calculate the current.

**Solution:**
$$I = \frac{Q}{t} = \frac{30}{10} = 3\,\text{A}$$

**Answer: 3 A.**

## Worked Example 2 (Easy–Medium)

**Question:** A current of 0.5 A flows through a lamp for 2 minutes. How much charge passes through the lamp?

**Solution:**

Convert time to seconds: $t = 2 \times 60 = 120\,\text{s}$

$$Q = I \times t = 0.5 \times 120 = 60\,\text{C}$$

**Answer: 60 C.**

## Worked Example 3 (Medium)

**Question:** In a circuit, $4.0 \times 10^{19}$ electrons pass a point every second. Calculate the current. (Charge on one electron = $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C.)

**Solution:**

Total charge per second:
$$Q = \text{(number of electrons)} \times \text{(charge per electron)}$$
$$Q = 4.0 \times 10^{19} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 6.4\,\text{C}$$

Since this is the charge per second:
$$I = \frac{Q}{t} = \frac{6.4}{1} = 6.4\,\text{A}$$

**Answer: 6.4 A.**

## Worked Example 4 (MSCE style)

**Question:** A student sets up a circuit with a battery, a switch, and two bulbs in series. The ammeter reads 0.4 A. The student then opens the switch.
(a) What does the ammeter read now?
(b) Explain why.
(c) If the switch is closed again and one bulb is removed, what does the ammeter read?

**Solution:**

**(a)** 0 A.

**(b)** Opening the switch breaks the loop. For current to flow there must be a complete circuit. With no complete path, no charge can flow, so no current.

**(c)** 0 A. Removing the bulb also breaks the series loop — there is no path for charge to flow through the gap. Current stops everywhere in the circuit.

**Answer: (a) 0 A  (b) broken loop  (c) 0 A.**

# Full Worked Solutions

**Check Yourself**

1. Coulomb (C).
2. $I = Q/t$
3. From the negative terminal to the positive terminal (through the external circuit).
4. From positive to negative.
5. The circuit must be a complete (closed) loop.
6. The same. In a series circuit, charge cannot pile up; whatever flows in one part must flow in every part.
7. In series with the component. So that all the current flows through the ammeter.
8. $I = 120 / 40 = 3\,\text{A}$
9. $Q = I t = 2 \times (5 \times 60) = 600\,\text{C}$
10. False. Current is not consumed. The bulb converts electrical energy into light and heat; the current is the same before and after.

**Q1.** Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge. $I = Q/t$.

**Q2.** $t = 60\,\text{s}$. $I = 240 / 60 = 4\,\text{A}$.

**Q3.** $t = Q / I = 15 / 0.25 = 60\,\text{s} = 1\,\text{minute}$.

**Q4.** Total charge:
$$Q = 2.5 \times 10^{20} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 40\,\text{C}$$
Current:
$$I = Q/t = 40 / 4 = 10\,\text{A}$$

**Q5.**
(a) In series with the bulb. So that the full current through the bulb also flows through the ammeter.
(b) 0 A. Opening the switch breaks the circuit, so no complete path exists and no current can flow.

**Q6.** In a series circuit there is only one path. Charge cannot be created, destroyed, or stored in the wires, so the same amount of charge must pass every point in the same time. Hence current is the same everywhere.

**Q7.** Mistake: current is not "used up." Correct statement: *"The bulb uses energy, not current. The current before and after the bulb is the same."*

**Q8.** $I = Q/t = 3 / 0.5 = 6\,\text{A}$.

**Q9.** Any two of:
- Electrons flow from − to +; conventional current is drawn + to −.
- Electron flow is physical; conventional current is a historical convention.
- Electron flow direction is fixed by physics; conventional current is a sign convention.

**Q10.** $t = 30 \times 60 = 1800\,\text{s}$. $Q = I t = 0.4 \times 1800 = 720\,\text{C}$.

# Common Mistakes Box

- Saying "current is used up." **It is not.**
- Confusing current with voltage or charge. Keep the three separate.
- Writing $I = t/Q$ instead of $I = Q/t$.
- Forgetting to convert minutes to seconds.
- Putting the ammeter in parallel. **Always in series.**
- Thinking electrons flow from + to −. They flow from − to +.
- Saying "the battery gives current." The battery gives **voltage** (energy per charge). The current depends on the whole circuit.
